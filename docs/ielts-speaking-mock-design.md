# IELTS Speaking Mock Test — Design Thinking

Working document. Part 1 is the ground truth (how the real exam runs). Part 2 is first-principles
product thinking. Part 3 is the template/content model. Part 4 is the flow. Part 5 is edge cases.
Part 6 is feedback and banding.

Sources: three IELTS Advantage mock-test transcripts (band 9, band 8, band 9) in
`.context/attachments/`, plus the published Cambridge test format and band descriptors.

---

## 1. How the real test actually runs

One examiner, one candidate, one room, 11–14 minutes, audio-recorded. There is no second person.
The examiner works from a fixed script and holds a stopwatch. The candidate sees no timer, no
question list, and gets no feedback of any kind until (weeks later) a number.

### 1.0 Pre-test — Introduction and ID check (~30–60s)

> "Good morning. My name is ___. Can you tell me your full name, please?"
> "What can I call you?"
> "Can you tell me where you're from?"
> "Can I see your identification, please?"

Not separately scored. Its function is procedural — but it is also what tells the candidate's
nervous system that an exam has begun. **The ritual is the frame.**

### 1.1 Part 1 — Introduction and interview (4–5 min)

Three topic "frames", each with 3–4 questions. Roughly 9–12 questions total.

- Frame 1 is almost always drawn from a fixed set: **work or study, hometown, or home/accommodation.**
  It follows naturally from the ID check.
- Frames 2 and 3 are everyday topics: music, food, holidays, drinks, maps, bags, birthdays, animals,
  photography, outdoor activities. (All of these appear literally in the three transcripts.)
- Scripted handoffs: *"Let's talk about music."* … *"Now let's move on to magazines and newspapers."*
- Expected answer length: **20–30 seconds. Two to four sentences.** Not longer. The examiner will cut
  you off if you run long, because the clock is fixed.
- Questions are simple, personal, present-tense-heavy, with a couple of past and future/preference
  questions mixed in to sample tense range.

The failure mode the examiners flag here is **listing**:

> "We asked you about your favourite food and you're like *I like Thai, I like Vietnamese, I like
> this*… the problem with that is it doesn't give the examiner enough to really judge your grammar.
> It's just present simple, present simple, present simple."

And **implicit answering**:

> "The first question I asked you, *how often do you listen to music*, and you told me *when* you
> listen to music. You're answering the question implicitly. You should answer each question
> explicitly."

### 1.2 Part 2 — The long turn (3–4 min)

The scripted handover, near-verbatim:

> "Now I'm going to give you a topic and I'd like you to talk about it for one to two minutes.
> Before you talk you'll have one minute to think about what you're going to say. You can make some
> notes if you wish. Do you understand?"

Card + paper + pencil are physically handed over. Then **60 seconds of silent prep**. Then:

> "All right? Remember you have one to two minutes for this, so don't worry if I stop you. I'll tell
> you when the time is up. Can you start speaking now, please."

Candidate speaks **1–2 minutes**, uninterrupted, keeping the card and their notes in front of them.
At 2:00 the examiner cuts them off mid-sentence — in one transcript, literally: *"…which is sweet
semolina situation — that's the end of the two minutes."*

Then **1–2 short rounding-off questions** ("Do you still see her?" / "Have you been back there?"),
answered in a sentence or two.

Cue card format is rigid:

```
Describe a city you would like to visit.

You should say:
  where it is
  how you would travel there
  what you would do there

and explain why you would like to visit this city.
```

Three "you should say" bullets, one "and explain" bullet. The fourth bullet is always the
why/how-you-felt one and is where band 7+ candidates spend most of their two minutes.

**The hidden trap in cue cards is tense.** The examiner spends three minutes of feedback on exactly
this:

> "*Describe a city you would like to visit* is normally a city you haven't been to — so that would
> be more talking about conditionals, hypotheticals, or the future… Some examiners might be like,
> *she's basically saying she doesn't know how to use future structures and she's playing it very
> safe*."

Every card implies a grammatical target. Encode it.

### 1.3 Part 3 — Two-way discussion (4–5 min)

> "We've been talking about a friend you met, and I'd now like to discuss with you one or two more
> general questions relating to this."

Abstract, impersonal discussion thematically downstream of the Part 2 card. Card was "a friend you
met" → Part 3 is "friends at school" then "making friends online" then "friendship and age".
Typically **2–3 sub-themes, 2–3 questions each, 5–7 questions total.** Answers run 40–60+ seconds.

Question types escalate along a fixed ladder:

| Frame | Example |
|---|---|
| Opinion | "How important is it for children to have lots of friends at school?" |
| Comparison | "Is it better to grow up in a city or the countryside?" |
| Advantage/disadvantage | "What are the downsides of living in a mega-city?" |
| Past vs present | "How strong a tradition is it *today* to go to the theatre?" |
| Prediction | "How will large cities in the future be different to how they are today?" |
| Policy/hypothetical | "Should theatre be run as a business or a public service?" |

**The single most useful mechanic in the whole exam, and it is adaptive:**

> "If you get a really really difficult question in Part 3 you should be happy, because the examiner
> thinks you're good. If the examiner thinks you're bad they'll just throw easy softball questions
> at you. If they think you're really good they'll throw more and more difficult questions to
> stretch you."

The examiner escalates difficulty in response to performance. This is not optional flavour — it's
load-bearing. **A candidate who is never stretched cannot demonstrate band 8–9.** Any mock that asks
everyone the same fixed Part 3 has a band ceiling baked into it.

And the corresponding candidate behaviour that gets rewarded:

> "Most students go *um, I don't know, I have no idea*… You gave a guess, you gave some kind of an
> answer, which is a plus point. Your attempt at an answer, even though it was quite short,
> indicates that you are at a very high level."

### 1.4 Closing

> "Thank you. That is the end of the speaking test."

No score, no comment, no reaction.

### 1.5 The examiner's affect — important, and counter-intuitive

The friendliest thing in these transcripts is a warning about friendliness:

> "You're a very friendly chatty person who wants the other person to interact — *girls like buying
> bags, right?* That is fine in normal conversation. If you were to do the test for real, the
> examiner could be quite stone-faced… on test day you might not get a very friendly examiner…
> Students have said they thought the examiner was criticising them or being rude, they took that to
> heart, and they went from doing very well to everything falling apart."

The examiner does not nod encouragingly, does not say "great answer", does not laugh at your joke,
does not answer questions you ask them. That neutrality is *itself* part of what students need to
rehearse. An AI teacher who is warm and affirming during the test is not a mock test — it is a
tutoring session wearing a mock test's clothes.

### 1.6 Marking

Four criteria, **equally weighted at 25% each**, averaged, then rounded to the nearest half band
(x.25 → x.5, x.75 → next whole).

| Criterion | What's actually being judged |
|---|---|
| **Fluency & Coherence** | Speaking at length without effort; *did you answer the question*; did you develop it; connectives used flexibly |
| **Lexical Resource** | Precision, topic-specific vocabulary, natural idiom, paraphrase, non-repetition |
| **Grammatical Range & Accuracy** | Range of structures + tense-switching + error density (slips vs. breakdowns) |
| **Pronunciation** | Intelligibility, intonation, sentence/word stress, connected speech |

Three things the transcripts make concrete that the descriptors don't:

1. **Fluency and coherence share one band, and the lower one wins.** Band 9 fluency + band 8
   coherence = band 8.
2. **Hesitation is scored by cause, not by amount.** Pausing to search for a *word* is penalised.
   Pausing to formulate an *idea* is not — it's evidence of high level. "A good examiner knows the
   difference between those two things." Any automated fluency metric that just counts pauses per
   minute will penalise exactly the band-8 behaviour it should reward.
3. **Accent is not penalised, at all.** Said explicitly in both feedback sessions. "You don't get
   higher marks for sounding British or American. You get marks for clarity." Indian users assume
   the opposite. Say it out loud in the product.

Also: **grammar degrades under cognitive load**, and examiners know it. "Your brain is like a
computer — if you have too many programs open, it slows down. If you're really thinking hard about
AI and cities, the part of your brain dealing with articles can slip." Article and preposition slips
cluster on the hard Part 3 questions. That's a feedback insight worth surfacing directly.

## 2. First principles: what actually produces the mock-test feeling

Strip away the surface. A mock test feels real when these hold:

1. **Irreversibility.** One take. No pause, no retry, no undo. The instant a user knows they can
   restart, the stakes evaporate and it becomes practice.
2. **Unknown content.** No preview of questions, no topic list, no hints, no vocabulary help.
3. **Externally-owned clock.** The time belongs to the examiner. You don't control when it moves on.
   Being cut off mid-sentence is the most exam-like sensation in the whole product.
4. **An evaluator who is present but neutral.** Judgment is being formed and withheld.
5. **Ritual and script.** The ID check, "do you understand?", the fixed handoffs, "that is the end of
   the speaking test." Ceremony is what tells the body this is different from a lesson.
6. **A consequence you didn't control.** A band, arrived at by someone else, delivered at the end.
7. **A cost to leaving.** Walking out mid-exam forfeits the result.

### Anti-goals

Everything below breaks the frame and must be absent from the test itself, however good it is
elsewhere in Stimuler: streaks, XP, progress bars, "you're doing great!", hints, vocabulary
suggestions, live scoring, retry buttons, sound effects on a good answer, confetti, an avatar that
smiles and nods approvingly.

Warmth is not banned from the product. It is **quarantined to the briefing and the feedback**. Sara
can be entirely human before the test starts and after it ends. In between, she is an examiner.

### The commitment problem

Points 1 and 7 are the hard ones, because a user can always background an app. You cannot enforce
irreversibility, so you have to **manufacture commitment**:

- A pre-test gate that states the terms plainly: *14 minutes. No pausing. No retries. You'll get your
  band at the end.* Make the user tap through it.
- A persistent "test in progress" state that is visually distinct from every other screen in the app.
- Abandonment produces an **incomplete attempt saved to the record**, not nothing. It shows up in
  history. Users should feel a small cost.

### Sara and the examiner: two people, two places

Sara hosts. The examiner examines. They never appear together and they never share a space.

- **Sara owns the lobby.** App-native, Stimuler brand, warm. She explains the format, sets the terms,
  runs the equipment check, and walks you to the door. After the test she is waiting outside it.
- **The examiner owns the room.** No name in the UI, no personality, no appearance anywhere else in
  Stimuler. You meet them here and nowhere else. That scarcity is what gives them authority.

This is better than one avatar switching register, because it turns the transition into a **change of
place** rather than a change of mood. A door is a stronger ritual than a tone shift, and it gives the
user somewhere to be nervous *before* and somewhere to decompress *after*.

The two spaces must look and behave like different products:

| | Lobby (Sara) | Room (examiner) |
|---|---|---|
| Chrome | Full app nav, back button | **None.** Full bleed. No nav, no back, no status chrome |
| Colour | Warm, brand | Cool, desaturated, flat |
| Framing | Casual, close, off-centre | Formal, straight-on, mid-shot, seated behind a desk |
| Affect | Warm, encouraging | Neutral, attentive, unreactive |
| Persistent UI | Normal | A single red recording dot |
| Can you leave? | Yes | Only through a confirmation that names the cost |

Removing the app chrome is what makes it feel like the user left Stimuler. It is the cheapest and
highest-leverage realism decision in the whole design.

The examiner introduces themselves by name exactly once ("My name is Daniel") and is never named
again — exactly as in the real test, where you never think of the examiner by name.

### The governing rule

Realism and comfort will conflict constantly. The rule that settles every one of those arguments:

> **Inside the exam, realism wins. Outside the exam, comfort wins.**

Everything warm, helpful, encouraging, explanatory or fun belongs to Sara's lobby or the feedback
screen. Nothing helpful crosses the door.

### Design principles

1. **The seal.** Once the door closes, nothing enters or leaves: no hints, no scores, no progress
   indicators, no app chrome, no notifications. Everything helpful happens before or after.
2. **The clock belongs to the examiner.** Being cut off mid-sentence is the feature, not the bug. It
   is the single most exam-like sensation available to you.
3. **Ceremony, not instruction.** Realism comes from ritual — scripted lines, the physical handover
   of the card, the recording dot, "that is the end of the speaking test" — never from the app
   explaining what is happening. Explanations belong to Sara.
4. **Attention without approval.** The examiner listens closely and gives nothing back. No nods of
   agreement, no smiles, no "good". Being attended to and not reassured is the specific stress
   students need to rehearse.
5. **Difficulty adapts, invisibly.** Part 3 escalates for strong candidates and stays gentle for weak
   ones, exactly as a real examiner does. The user must never see the throttle.
6. **The struggle is recorded, not rescued.** Every fumble is captured as evidence and becomes the
   feedback. Nothing is fixed in the moment.
7. **Evidence over advice.** Feedback quotes the user's own audio with a play button. "You tend to
   list" is worth nothing next to hearing yourself list.
8. **The recording is the artifact.** The replayable test is a large part of what the user is buying.
   Never lose it — buffer locally, upload later, degrade to audio-only before you drop it.
9. **Honest bands.** These users get a real score eventually. Inflation is a trust bomb with a long
   fuse. Below the evidence threshold, refuse to score rather than invent one.

### Where the fun actually comes from

Not from gamification — the fun of a mock test is the fun of a **dare**, not the fun of a game.

- **Nerves.** Stakes are enjoyable when they're safe. The whole point is a controlled dose of the
  real thing.
- **The draw.** Not knowing which cue card you'll get is a genuine surprise beat, like turning over
  a card.
- **Survival.** Finishing a hard thing feels good on its own, independent of the score.
- **The reveal.** A band you didn't control, delivered at the end, is the payoff the entire 14
  minutes has been building.
- **Hearing yourself.** Unreasonably compelling, and it is where the learning lands.
- **Beating your last one.** Progression across attempts, which is what turns a one-off event into a
  habit.

Every one of these is destroyed by adding XP, streaks, or encouragement to the test itself.

### Positioning on the roadmap

The full 14-minute mock is a heavy, high-stakes object with real emotional cost. It should be a
**checkpoint every N lessons, not a daily habit.** Daily work is short part-specific drills (a single
Part 2 card, one Part 1 frame) in Practice Mode. The full mock is the milestone you take to find out
where you stand.

Give users a ladder in, gated on self-reported level:
`Part 1 only (5 min)` → `Part 1 + 2 (8 min)` → `Full mock (14 min)`.
Putting a band-4 speaker through a full mock is demoralising and teaches almost nothing.

### Two modes, strictly separated

| | **Exam Mode** | **Practice Mode** |
|---|---|---|
| Hints / vocab help | None | Yes |
| Retry an answer | No | Yes |
| Prep time | Only Part 2's 60s | Configurable |
| Feedback | End only | Per answer |
| Interruptions | Yes | No |
| Sara's affect | Neutral | Encouraging |
| Produces a band | Yes | No |

They must not blend. The mock test's value comes entirely from being sealed.

## 3. The template / content model

This is the part that has to generalise across every topic you'll ever author.

```
MockTestSet
  id
  difficulty_tier          # affects Part 3 abstraction, not Part 1
  theme_family_part1[]     # must not intersect theme_family_part23
  theme_family_part23
  part1: TopicFrame[3]
  part2: CueCardTask
  part3: DiscussionCluster[2..3]
  time_budget_sec: 660..840
```

### Part 1 — TopicFrame ×3

```
TopicFrame
  topic_id                 # "music", "hometown", "food"
  is_opener                # frame 1 only; drawn from {work_or_study, hometown, home}
  transition_line          # "Let's talk about music." | "Now let's move on to X."
  questions: Question[3..4]

Question
  text
  repeat_text              # verbatim identical — examiners repeat, never rephrase, in Part 1
  tense_target             # present_habit | past | future | preference | comparison
  expected_len_sec         # 20..30
  is_droppable             # the 4th question in a frame is droppable when running long
  probe                    # optional, max one per frame: "Why is that?"
```

**Rules:** exactly 3 frames. Frame 1 is the identity-adjacent one. Minimum 2 questions asked per
frame (a frame with 1 question doesn't sample the topic). Frames must collectively cover at least
one past-tense and one future/preference question — otherwise you haven't sampled tense range and
your GRA score is under-evidenced.

### Part 2 — CueCardTask

```
CueCardTask
  prompt_stem              # "Describe a city you would like to visit."
  bullets: [3]             # rendered under "You should say:"
  explain_bullet           # rendered as "and explain ..."
  topic_family             # person | place | object | event | experience | time_when | abstract
  tense_target             # THE trap. e.g. "would like to visit" => conditional + future
  prep_sec: 60
  speak_min_sec: 60
  speak_max_sec: 120
  rounding_off: Question[1..2]
  bridge_line              # "We've been talking about X, and I'd now like to discuss ..."
```

**Content QA rules for cue cards** (these are the ones that will bite you at scale):

- Answerable by someone who has never left their hometown, has no money, and has no unusual
  experiences. Real Cambridge cards are engineered for universal answerability.
- No culturally loaded content: alcohol, dating, religion, politics, family conflict.
- No socioeconomic assumptions: "describe a car you own", "describe a holiday abroad".
- No terms with contested definitions. The transcript spends ninety seconds on whether Bali is a
  "city" — "in some places a city is a big built-up area with millions of people, in others a city
  is basically not the countryside." If a word is ambiguous across cultures, don't build a card
  around it.
- `tense_target` must be explicitly authored, not inferred at scoring time.

### Part 3 — DiscussionCluster ×2–3

```
DiscussionCluster
  sub_theme
  questions: Question3[2..3]

Question3
  text
  cognitive_frame          # opinion | comparison | adv_disadv | past_vs_present
                           # | prediction | causation | policy | hypothetical
  difficulty_rank          # 1..3 — drives adaptive escalation
  probe                    # "Why do you think that is?" / "Could you give an example?"
  rephrase_text            # Part 3 permits ONE rephrase (Part 1 does not)
```

**Adaptive escalation.** Author 3 difficulty ranks per cluster; pick live based on a running quality
signal (answer length, lexical variety, hesitation type, coherence). Strong performance → rank 3.
Weak performance → stay at rank 1. This mirrors the real examiner and, critically, is what makes
band 8–9 reachable at all. It also solves the "user is struggling" problem without breaking the
frame — the test gets survivable without ever offering help.

Do not surface the escalation to the user. They should never know they're being throttled.

### Timing envelope

| Segment | Budget |
|---|---|
| Intro + ID check | 0:30–1:00 |
| Part 1 | 4:00–5:00 |
| Part 2 instructions | 0:30 |
| Part 2 prep | 1:00 |
| Part 2 long turn | 1:00–2:00 |
| Part 2 rounding off | 0:30 |
| Part 3 | 4:00–5:00 |
| Close | 0:15 |
| **Total** | **11:00–14:00** |

**The test is time-boxed, not question-boxed.** A user who gives 60-second Part 1 answers gets fewer
questions — exactly as in real life. The question list is a pool with a floor; the engine pulls until
the budget is spent, dropping `is_droppable` questions first and interrupting over-long answers.

### Content production cost

Per set: ~12 Part 1 questions + 1 card (4 bullets) + 2 rounding-off + 9 Part 3 questions ≈ **24
authored questions**, of which 16–20 get asked. For no repeats across 10 attempts you need ~10 sets
≈ 240 questions, plus per-user topic history so cards and topics don't recur within N attempts.

Practice Mode should deliberately allow re-taking the *same* set, so users can compare against
themselves.

## 4. The user flow, screen by screen

Two spaces: **the lobby** (Sara, app-native) and **the room** (examiner, full bleed). The door
between them is the most important transition in the product.

### The lobby — Sara

| # | Screen | Sara says / user does | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Roadmap card | "Mock Speaking Test — 14 min". Shows last band if any. | — |
| 2 | Terms gate | 14 minutes. No pausing. No retries. One take. Band at the end. User taps through. | 10s |
| 3 | Setup check | Mic permission (hard block), headphone prompt, ambient noise floor (warn only), network. | 20s |
| 4 | Level select | Target band + "sat IELTS before?" → offers the ladder (Part 1 only / Parts 1+2 / full) if low. | 15s |
| 5 | **Sara's briefing** | The three parts, how long each is, that the examiner will not help or react, that thinking pauses are fine, that she'll be waiting when it's done. | ~45s |
| 6 | `[ I'm ready ]` | The last friendly button in the flow. | — |

### The door

App chrome peels away. Colour cools. Sara's voice fades out. A beat of near-silence — 1.5 to 2
seconds of nothing, which is the most valuable dead air in the product. The room fades up: examiner
seated behind a desk, straight-on mid-shot, a red recording dot appears. No nav bar, no back button.

### The room — the examiner

| # | Segment | What's on screen | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | ID check | Examiner only. Doubles as mic calibration — "your full name" is both the real opening line and the audio level test. | 0:45 |
| 8 | Part 1 | Examiner only. **No timer, no question counter.** A counter would tell you how much is left; real candidates don't know. | 4:30 |
| 9 | Part 2 instructions | Examiner only. Scripted verbatim, ending "Do you understand?" | 0:30 |
| 10 | Part 2 prep | **Card slides in from the examiner's side of the desk** + blank notepad. Visible 60s countdown. `[ Start speaking now ]` to skip. Spellcheck/autocorrect/autocomplete **off**. | 1:00 |
| 11 | Part 2 long turn | Notepad locks read-only; **card and notes stay visible**. Examiner listens, silent, unreactive. No visible timer. Hard cut at 2:00 mid-sentence: "Thank you." | 1:00–2:00 |
| 12 | Rounding off | Card slides away. 1–2 short follow-ups. | 0:30 |
| 13 | Part 3 | Bridge line, then 2–3 clusters with silent adaptive difficulty. Longer endpointing threshold than Part 1. | 4:30 |
| 14 | Close | "Thank you. That is the end of the speaking test." Recording dot goes out. Hard stop, no score, no comment. | 0:15 |

Mobile layout risk: in step 10, the keyboard will cover the cue card. Card must stay pinned above a
bottom-sheet notepad.

### Back to the lobby — Sara

| # | Screen | Sara says / user does |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | The door out | Room fades, chrome returns, warmth returns. Sara is waiting. |
| 16 | **Sara's outro** | Decompression, ~20s. Acknowledges it's over and that it's normal to feel it went badly. Explicitly not a score. Then hands over. |
| 17 | Scoring | Progress state. If it takes >30s, show the transcript and playback while the band computes. |
| 18 | Results | Band + four criteria + arithmetic shown. Per-part commentary. Per-criterion with **playable evidence clips**. Your Part 2 notes, shown next to what you actually said. Trend vs. previous attempts. |
| 19 | Recovery | "Practise these" → the exact questions you fumbled, in Practice Mode: prep time, model answers, phrase banks, retries. All learning support lives here. |

## 5. Use cases, edge cases, error cases

### 5.1 The candidate-behaviour cases — handle these *in character*

| Case | Real examiner behaviour | Product behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Silence after a question | Waits ~5–8s, offers one repeat, moves on | Same. Never a hint. |
| "Sorry, can you repeat that?" | Repeats **verbatim**, once | Repeat `repeat_text` exactly. Second request → repeat once more, then move on. Log it. |
| "What does *X* mean?" | In Part 1: **cannot explain.** In Part 3: may rephrase once | Part 1: *"I'm sorry, I can't explain that. Let's move on."* Part 3: use `rephrase_text` once. This is the most exam-authentic moment in the product. |
| Asks Sara a question ("what do you think?") | Deflects | *"I'm afraid I can't answer that. Let's continue."* |
| One-word answers | Doesn't beg for more; may probe once | Max one `probe` per Part 1 frame. |
| Rambles past budget | Cuts off | Soft interrupt: *"Thank you."* after N sec over. |
| Off-topic / wrong-topic in Part 2 | Says nothing; marks it down | Say nothing. Score under coherence. Surface in feedback. |
| Under 1:00 in Part 2 | One nudge, then ends turn | Same. |
| "I don't know" on a hard Part 3 question | Moves on | Move on. Note in feedback that *attempting* scores higher than declining — the transcripts are emphatic about this. |
| Code-switches to L1 | Continues, marks it down | No correction in-test. Flag in feedback. |
| Unintelligible speech | Continues, marks pronunciation down | Continue. Never tell them mid-test. |
| Obviously memorised/recited answer | Heavily penalised | Detect (abnormally low disfluency + flat prosody + high lexical density + suspiciously on-topic) and flag in feedback. This is a real IELTS penalty and would differentiate you. |
| Laughs, coughs, sighs, "um" | Ignores | Must not be treated as a turn boundary. |
| Second voice in the room | Would invalidate | Detect, warn post-test, flag score confidence. |

### 5.2 Interruption and abandonment — the commitment cases

- **Backgrounded < ~20s (call, notification):** pause clock, one grace, resume with *"Let's
  continue."*
- **Backgrounded longer, or repeatedly:** terminate as **incomplete**. Save partial audio, transcript
  and per-part notes. Withhold the final band, or show it explicitly labelled *indicative,
  incomplete*.
- **User taps End Test:** confirmation dialog naming the cost, then save as abandoned.
- **Screen lock / battery:** keep audio capture alive; screen-off should not end the test.
- **Network drop mid-test:** the test must continue locally. Buffer audio to disk, upload later,
  never lose the recording. Sara's next line should be pre-fetched a question ahead.

### 5.3 The technical failure cases

- **Mic permission denied** → hard block at the lobby.
- **Input level far too low** → caught at the ID check, in character, before anything is scored.
- **Speaker output + echo** → Sara's voice bleeds into the transcript and corrupts scoring. Need AEC;
  if AEC confidence is low, prompt for headphones in the lobby.
- **Avatar/video fails to render** → fall back to audio-only with a static frame. Never block the
  test on video.
- **ASR fails while audio is fine** → user's speech is unintelligible; that's a *score* outcome, not
  an error. Do not surface.
- **Audio is silent/broken while user believes they're speaking** → this *is* an error and must be
  surfaced immediately, or you've wasted 14 minutes of a stressed user's time. This distinction
  (bad speech vs. bad capture) is critical and non-trivial.
- **Scoring service fails post-test** → show transcript and playback with "score pending" rather than
  nothing.
- **Upload fails** → local retry queue, resumable.

### 5.4 The scoring-validity cases

Set a **minimum evidence threshold** — roughly ≥90 seconds of scorable user speech and ≥8 answered
questions. Below it, show *"not enough speech to produce a reliable band"* with a per-criterion
partial read. Producing a confident-looking 5.5 from forty seconds of audio is the fastest way to
lose an IELTS user's trust, because they will eventually get a real score to compare against.

Show a **band range** rather than a point when confidence is low ("6.0–6.5").

### 5.5 The engineering risk that decides whether this works: endpointing

Deciding "the user has finished their answer" is the make-or-break problem.

A naive VAD with a 1.5s silence cutoff will interrupt people who pause to think. But pausing to
formulate an idea is *exactly the behaviour scored as band 8–9* ("you were trying to formulate your
thoughts and come up with the appropriate precise word, which you did — that demonstrates a very
high level of vocabulary"). A dumb cutoff punishes your best users and makes Sara feel rude.

- Combine silence duration with whether the utterance is syntactically/semantically complete.
- Longer thresholds in Part 3 (~2.5–3.0s) than Part 1 (~1.8–2.0s). Part 2 has no endpointing at all —
  it's a fixed 2-minute turn.
- Falling intonation and syntactic completeness are stronger end-of-turn signals than silence alone.
- Target 0.8–1.5s from user-stop to Sara's next line. Instant feels robotic; >2s feels broken.
- No barge-in in Part 1/3 — examiners aren't interrupted — but keep the mic hot so the transcript
  is complete.

## 6. Feedback and banding

Structure the feedback the way the examiner does it in the transcripts, because that structure is
proven and users recognise it as legitimate.

**1. The band.** Overall, large, plus four criteria bands. **Show the arithmetic** — "7.0, 7.0, 6.5,
6.5 → average 6.75 → rounded to 7.0". Showing the rounding rule makes the number feel earned rather
than generated.

**2. Per-part commentary.** Part 1 / Part 2 / Part 3, each: what you did, and what to watch on test
day. This is where behavioural feedback lives, separate from criterion feedback.

**3. Per-criterion, with evidence.** This is the feature that matters most: **playable clips from
their own audio**, timestamped, with the transcript. Not "you tend to list" but *"at 4:32 you said
'I like Thai, I like Vietnamese, I like Korean' — [▶] — this is listing."* Nothing else you can build
is as persuasive as a person hearing themselves do the thing.

**4. Named, fixable behaviours.** From the transcripts, these are the recurring ones and they should
be a taxonomy you detect against:

- Listing instead of developing (Part 1)
- Answering implicitly — "how often" answered with *when* instead of a frequency word
- Not matching the tense the cue card demands
- Not answering the exact cue card (asked for actor **or** actress, gave both)
- Declining hard Part 3 questions instead of attempting
- Article and preposition slips clustering on cognitively hard questions
- Repeating the same idiom / using imprecise stock phrases
- Over-interacting with the examiner
- Memorised-sounding answers

**5. "What moves you up half a band."** One or two things, ranked, actionable. Not a list of nine.

**6. Comparison to previous attempts.** Per-criterion trend. This is what makes the mock a checkpoint
rather than an event.

**7. The recovery loop.** A "practise these" handoff that takes the exact questions they struggled
with into Practice Mode — with prep time, model answers, phrase banks, retries. **All learning
support lives here**, downstream of the sealed test. That is the complete answer to "what if the user
can't speak properly": nothing during, everything after.

### Non-negotiables

- **No score, no hint, no signal of any kind during the test.**
- **Be honest when the band is low.** These users will get a real score eventually. Inflation is a
  trust bomb with a long fuse.
- **Say explicitly that accent is not penalised**, standing text in the pronunciation section. Both
  feedback sessions stress it. Indian users assume the opposite and it distorts how they practise.
- **Label it an AI estimate**, with a confidence indicator.
- **Score hesitation by cause, not by count.** A fluency metric that counts pauses per minute will
  mark down exactly the behaviour examiners reward.

## 7. What I'd prototype first

**Part 2 alone.** Cue card handover → 60s prep with notes → 2-minute turn → hard cut → rounding off.

It's the most distinctive part of the experience, the most self-contained, has all the novel UI (card
handover, notepad, countdown, lock), and exercises the hardest mechanics (strict timing, cutting a
user off mid-sentence, long-form ASR, notes-as-feedback-artifact). If the hard cut at 2:00 feels
thrilling rather than rude, the whole product works. If it feels rude, you've learned the most
important thing you could learn, in three minutes instead of fourteen.

Second slice: Part 1 with three frames, to shake out endpointing and turn latency, which is where the
realism actually lives or dies.

## Open questions

1. **Visible timers.** Part 2 prep countdown — yes, defensible. During the 2-minute turn — I'd say
   no, but it's testable and the anxiety cost may be higher than the realism gain.
2. **Repeat allowance.** Real examiners repeat once. Do we cap it, or let struggling users repeat
   freely and just record the count as a signal?
3. **Adaptive escalation transparency.** Should the feedback ever tell the user "you were asked
   easier questions because…"? Honest, but potentially crushing.
4. **Mobile layout for Part 2** — the keyboard covering the cue card is the single biggest layout
   risk. Card pinned above a bottom-sheet notepad is my instinct.
5. **Does the avatar take notes on camera?** Real examiners do, and it's a genuine stressor. Might be
   the highest realism-per-unit-effort detail in the whole design.
